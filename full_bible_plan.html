    An engine for a year of ministrations

    One Bible, three passes, fifty-two weeks at a time. The plan below is built so that a whole church - not only its teachers - arrives at the end of three years owning the shape, the books and the argument of Scripture, and able to hand it to somebody else.

    Year 1 · The Story

    Genesis to Revelation, whole chapters, in the order the church received them. Five short sittings a week. The only demand is that nobody stops at Leviticus.

    You will own the plot. Every member can place any book and tell any story.

    Year 2 · The Book

    One book a week, read whole and then taken apart: occasion, structure, argument, key word, hard question, and a ten-minute teaching you give to somebody.

    You will read documents, not verses. Proof-texting dies in a week inside a book.

    Year 3 · The Thread

    Fifty-two movements from creation to the city: promise and fulfilment in two columns, by hand, all year.

    You will think in the whole. The gospel stops sounding like an interruption.

    How a week runs

    • Mon–Fri: one portion each day, five to twenty minutes. Read it aloud if anyone is within earshot.
    • At the end of each portion: close the book and tell it back in three minutes.
    • Saturday: the week's verse written out by hand, then said without the page.
    • The night between: a member of the body teaches the week's portion for twelve minutes, in a house, and asks the three questions aloud - see .
    • Sunday: the pulpit takes the same text the church has been eating; the answer to the hard question is read out.
    • Once a month: a sabbath of reading - one portion, three times, slowly, with nothing counted.

    Three rules carry the whole year. Break them and the plan becomes paper.

    1. Never skip a hard book. The difficult chapters get the same chair at the table.
    2. Catch up by doubling, never by stretching. Miss a week: read two portions a day for a week. The year is fixed; the schedule is flexible.
    3. Nothing is finished until it is handed on. A person, a sentence, this week - at least once.
    Seriousness, said plainly. Each year of this plan is a year's work - roughly 1,189 chapters across the three passes, one book a week, one thread a month. It is not a devotional luxury. Set the start Sunday in and expect to arrive at the other end of the Bible, not merely nearer it.

    For generations: a cycle, not a calendar

    Three years covers the whole Bible from the pulpit - 156 Sundays, 66 books, 1,189 chapters. Then it begins again, and the second time the church does more of the work. Nothing here expires, nothing is hosted, and no year is written into the file: the only date is the Sunday you choose.

    The perpetual rules

    1. The cycle is the plan. Do not invent a new series every January; begin the next pass, at the next depth, with the same texts.
    2. The year is fixed, the schedule is flexible. A Sunday lost to illness or a journey is folded into the next week's reading, never added to the end of the year.
    3. Every pass is registered. Set the pass number in Settings; it prints on the year sheet, so the record says "Pass 2, Year 4" and a reader in 2040 knows exactly where they are.
    4. The notebook is the archive. Export the year's CSV at its close and keep it with the church's minutes; a ministry's memory is a pile of one-line notes.
    5. Hand it on while it is still working. The plan is successful when somebody else can run Year 1 without you.

    Strategies that teach the Bible deeply

    Twelve moves, each one small enough to run in a household and strong enough to carry a pulpit. They are attached to specific weeks below, but they are not secret: teach the church the method and the church keeps the book.

    Why these work

    Retrieval beats re-reading. Recalling a thing changes the memory of it more than reading it again; the recall panels in each week card exist for that reason, and so does the quarterly test.

    Spacing beats cramming. A review at one day, one week and one month is worth more than four hours in one sitting; the plan therefore never lets a quarter finish without returning to it.

    Structure before detail. A reader who can draw a book's movements can hang verses somewhere. Every week in Year 2 begins with the outline, not with the commentary.

    Words carry thought. Hesed, emunah, pleroma, basileia - a church that has tracked five uses of a word cannot be moved by a slogan.

    Output is the examination. Nothing has been learned until it has been taught to one person in ten minutes. Every year ends each week that way.

    Reading becomes prayer or it becomes pride. Each portion closes with four movements of prayer, so knowledge arrives with knees.

    The calendar, and how the plan bends without breaking

    A church year is not a school year, and neither is a farm year. The plan assumes interruption - and tells you exactly what to do when it arrives.

    Interruptions: the four rules

    1. Feasts take the text. Advent, Epiphany, Lent, Good Friday, Easter, Pentecost and the church's own gatherings read their own Scripture; the plan pauses and resumes the following Monday, without a makeup week.
    2. Examination weeks halve the portions. In the institution's exam weeks, the household reads one portion, not five, and recalls the quarter instead.
    3. Absence keeps the count. Two weeks away: put both halves on the table at once - 40 chapters in a week is not piety, it is arithmetic; if that is impossible, read one portion a day for a fortnight and lose the pace, never the year.
    4. The year ends on time. Week 52 arrives whatever happened. A short 'Review and catch-up' week is built in for every quarter's last day, and reading is not a debt but a diet.

    Monthly ministrations, matched to the readings

    What the preacher carries from a week

    Each week card gives the portion, the book's movement, the word the book keeps using, and the difficulty the book will not let go of. A sermon that names all four in the first four minutes has told the church where it is in the Bible - and the church will remember it longer than any illustration.

    The order for the study: read the portion six times before the tools are opened; write the one sentence the text says; write the one sentence the congregation must obey; then - and only then - the commentaries; then the prayer for the room.

    The hard question belongs in the pulpit. Not as a scandal, but as an honest sentence: 'this is what people ask about Habakkuk, and this is what the text will and will not answer'. A church taught to bring its questions to the text stops taking them elsewhere.
